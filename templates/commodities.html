ETF vs Futures — the 60-second primer

Commodity ETF

A fund that holds physical metal (or its proxy) and issues units that trade on the stock exchange. You buy units like a share, hold them in your demat, and the unit price tracks the underlying metal minus a small expense ratio. There's no expiry, no leverage, no margin call. Best for someone who wants exposure to gold or silver as part of a portfolio and isn't interested in trading.

Futures Contract

A standardized agreement to buy/sell a fixed quantity at a fixed date. You pay a small margin (5–10% of notional) instead of full price — that's leverage. Every day your position is marked-to-market: gains credit your account, losses debit it. Contracts expire monthly, so to "hold" you must roll over by closing the expiring one and opening the next month — and pay the price difference (the "roll cost"). Best for active traders, hedgers, or short-term tactical bets.

The short version: for a long-term retail investor who wants to own a commodity for years, the ETF is almost always the right vehicle. Futures are powerful but they're a different sport — they exist for traders and hedgers, not buyers. Click any commodity above to see a full ETF-vs-Futures breakdown with current market data.

How each one actually works

The ETF route

You open your broker app and buy units of {% if spec.etf %}{{ spec.etf.ticker }}{% else %}(none available){% endif %} just like you'd buy a share. The fund house behind the ETF takes your money, buys the actual underlying ({{ spec.name|lower }} bullion in a vault for metals), and the unit price tracks the spot value of those holdings. You pay a small annual management fee (the expense ratio) which is automatically baked into the unit NAV — no separate bill. When you want to exit, you sell the units on the exchange. That's the entire lifecycle. No expiry, no rollover, no margin, no surprises.

The Futures route

You sign a contract to buy {{ spec.futures.lot_size }} {{ spec.futures.unit }} of {{ spec.name|lower }} on a specific future date at today's agreed price. To enter, you only post a margin (~5–10% of the notional value), so a relatively small amount of capital controls a much larger position — that's leverage cutting both ways. Every single trading day the exchange revalues your position: if the price moved against you, money is debited from your account that evening (mark-to-market). If your account falls below the maintenance margin, you get a margin call and must add money or your position is liquidated. Each contract expires monthly; to keep your exposure you "roll" by closing the expiring contract and opening the next month — and the next month is usually slightly more expensive (contango), so each rollover quietly costs you a fraction of a percent.
